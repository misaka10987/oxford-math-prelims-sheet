# Linear Algebra I — Sheet 3

1. Define vector addition $(u_n) + (v_n) = (u_n + v_n)$ .
   
   - Obviously this is commutative and associative.
   
   - $$
     \begin{align*}
(u+v)_{n+1} &= u_{n+1} + v_{n+1} 
\\ &= u_n + u_{n-1} + v_n + v_{n-1} 
\\ &= (u+v)_n + (u+v)_{n-1}
\end{align*}
     $$
     
     Therefore, closed.
   
   - The additive unit is constant zero sequence, and inverse is done by negation of $\R$ on each term.
   
   Define scalar multiplication $x \bullet (u_n) = (xu_n)$ .
   
   - $$
     \begin{align*}
(x \bullet u)_{n + 1} &= xu_{n + 1}
\\ & = x(u_n + u_{n-1})
\\ & = xu_n + xu_{n-1}
\\ & = (x \bullet u)_n + (x \bullet u)_{n-1}
\end{align*}
     $$
     
     Therefore, closed.
   
   - Associativity and identity under multiplication by $1$ is easy to verify.
   
   - Distribution to vector addition:
     
     $$
     \begin{align*}
x \bullet (u_n + v_n) & = (x (u_n+v_n)) 
\\ &= (xu_n + xv_n)
\\ & = (xu_n) + (xv_n)
\\ & = x \bullet (u_n) + x \bullet (v_n)
\end{align*}
     $$
   
   - Distribution to scalar addition:
     
     $$
     \begin{align*}
(x + y) \bullet (u_n)& = ((x + y)u_n)
\\ & = (xu_n + yu_n)
\\ & = (xu_n) + (yu_n)
\\ & = x \bullet (u_n) + y \bullet (u_n)
\end{align*}
     $$
   
   Therefore $(\left\{ (u_n) \right\}, +, \R, \bullet)$ is a vector space.
   
   Notice that a sequence $(u_n)$ is uniquely determined by $(u_1, u_2)$ . Thus, the two sequences generated by $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ are a basis, and the dimension is 2.

6. (i) Obviously $\mathbf 0 \in V_1$ . $\forall \mathbf v, \mathbf w \in V_1$ ,
   
   $$
   \begin{align*}
\sum_{1 \le i \le n} (\mathbf v + \mathbf w)_i
& = \sum_{1 \le i \le n} (\mathbf v_i + \mathbf w_i)
\\ & = \sum_{1 \le i \le n}\mathbf v_i +
\sum_{1 \le i \le n} \mathbf w_i
\\ & = 0
\end{align*}
   $$
   
   $$
   \sum_{1 \le i \le n} (c \mathbf v)_i
= \sum_{1 \le i \le n} c \mathbf v_i
= c \sum_{1 \le i \le n} \mathbf v_i
= 0
   $$
   
   Therefore $V_1$ is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication, thus it is a subspace.
   
   For $\R^0$ , $\left\{ * \right\}$ is a basis.
   
   For $\R^1$ , $\left\{ (0) \right\}$ is a basis.
   
   For $n \ge 2$ , Let $\mathbf v_m \in \R^n$ be the vector where the $m$ -th component is $n-1$ and all other components are $-1$ , then $\left\{ \mathbf v_i \mid 1 \le i \le n \right\}$ is a basis.
   
   (ii) Obviously $\mathbf 0 \in V_2$ . $\forall \mathbf v, \mathbf w \in V_2$ ,
   
   $$
   (\mathbf v + \mathbf w)_{i,j} = \mathbf v_{i,j} + \mathbf w_{i,j}
= \mathbf v_{j,i} + \mathbf w_{j,i} = (\mathbf v + \mathbf w)_{j,i}
   $$
   
   $$
   (c \mathbf v)_{i,j} = c\mathbf v_{i,j}
= c\mathbf v_{j,i} = (c\mathbf v)_{j,i}
   $$
   
   Therefore $V_2$ is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication, thus it is a subspace.
   
   Let $M_{i,j} \in \R^{n \times n}$ be the matrix where the entries $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$ are $1$ and $0$ elsewhere. Then $\left\{ M_{i,j} \mid 1 \le i \le j \le n \right\}$ is a basis.
   
   (iii) $\mathbf 0 \in V_3$ and closure under addition and multiplication can be verified similar to (ii). Let $M_{i,j}$ be the matrix where the entry $(i,j)$ is $1$ , $(j,i)$ is $-1$ , and $0$ elsewhere, then $\left\{ M_{i,j} \mid 1 \le i < j < n \right\}$ is a basis for $n \ge 2$ , $\left \{ (0) \right\}$ is a basis for $n = 1$ , and $\left\{ * \right\}$ is a basis for $n = 0$ .
