1. (i) By definition $o(\overline x)$ is the smallest integer such that $o(\overline x)x \equiv 0$ and that $n \mid o(\overline x) x$ . Then by definition $o(\overline x) x = \operatorname{lcm}(x,n)$ , and because $\operatorname{lcm}(x,n) = \frac{xn}{\gcd(x,n)}$ , $o(\overline x) = \frac{n}{\gcd(x,n)}$ . $\square$ 
   
   (ii) By definition $o(g,h)$ is the smallest integer such that $(g,h)^{o(g,h)} = 1$ and that $(g^{o(g,h)}, h^{o(g,h)}) = 1$ . Therefore $o(g,h)$ is the smallest integer such that $g^{o(g,h)} = h^{o(g,h)} = 1$ . Since $g^n = 1 \iff o(g) \mid n$ and $h^n = 1 \iff o(g) \mid n$ , by definition, $o(g,h) = \operatorname{lcm}(o(g),o(h))$ . $\square$

4. $\langle g \rangle \cap \langle h \rangle \le \langle g \rangle$ , therefore $|\langle g \rangle \cap \langle h \rangle| \mid |\langle g \rangle|$ and $|\langle g \rangle \cap \langle h \rangle| = 1$ or $p$ since $|g| = p$ is prime, and both are possible (consider $\langle1\rangle \cap \langle -1 \rangle \le \Z_3$ and $\langle(1,0) \rangle \cap \langle(0,1) \rangle \le {\Z_3}^2$ ).
   
   Since $|\langle g \rangle \cap \langle h \rangle| = 1$ or $p$ , $\langle g \rangle$ and $\langle h \rangle$ either be equal or intersect at only the identity for all elements $g,h$ of order $p$ . Consider the collection of all distinct groups generated by elements of order $p$ in $G$ . Then every element of order $p$ is included in some group in the collection. Since each group has $p-1$ generators all of order $p$ in $G$ and these groups do not intersect except for identity, the collection has a multiple of $p-1$ of elements of order $p$ and so is $G$ .
   
   If $G$ has an element of order 35, $G = \Z_{35}$ and has elements of order 5 and 7. Otherwise, let $4n$ be number of elements of order $5$ and $6m$ be number of elements of order $7$ . Then $4n + 7m = 34$ and $n,m$ cannot be both zero. $\square$ 

5. For $x,y \in G$ , $(xy)^2 = xyxy = e$ , and $x^2 y^2 = xxyy = e$ gives $xy = yx$ .
   
   For $k_1, k_2 \in K$ ,
   
   - If $k_1, k_2 \in H$ , $k_1k_2 \in H \subseteq K$ .
   
   - If $k_1 \in H$ , $k_2 \in gH$ , $k_1k_2 \in gH \subseteq K$ .
   
   - If $k_1,k_2 \in gH$ , $k_1k_2 \in g^2H = H \subseteq K$ .
   
   For $k \in K$ ,
   
   - If $k \in H$ , $k^{-1} \in H \subseteq K$ .
   
   - If $k \in gH$ , let $k=gh$ , then $k(gh^{-1}) = g^2 = 1$ and $k^{-1} = gh^{-1} \in gH \subseteq K$ .
   
   Therefore $K \le G$ .
   
   Obviously $H$ and $gH$ are disjoint. Then
   
   $$
   \phi: H \times \Z_2 \to K  = (h,x) \mapsto g^xh
   $$
   
   is an isomorphism.
   
   Choose $H = \{ e \}$ . Then there is a subgroup $K_1 \le G$ and $K_1 \cong H \times \Z_2 = \Z_2$ . As long as $K_n < G$ , we can repeat this step to get a larger subgroup $K_{n+1} \cong \Z_2 \times K_n \cong {\Z_{2}}^n$ . Since $G$ is finite the strictly increasing chain must terminate at some point where $K_n = G$ . $\square$ 

6. (i) If $h_1, h_2 \in H_1$ , then $h_1h_2 \in H_1$ since $(h_1h_2,e)=(h_1,e)(h_2,e) \in K$ .
   
   If $h \in H_1$ , then $h^{-1} \in H_1$ since $(h,e)^{-1}=(h^{-1},e) \in K$ .
   
   Therefore $H_1 \le G_1$ . Similarly so is $H_2$ . $H_1 \times H_2$ is a group with the same operation as $K$ and obviously every element is in $K$ because $(g_1, g_2) = (g_1, e) (e,g_2)$ . $\square$ 
   
   (ii) If $\gcd(|G_1|, |G_2|) = 1$ , for $(g_1, g_2) \in K$ , $(g_1,g_2)^{n} = (g_1, e)$ for $n \equiv 1 \mod |g_1|$ and $n \equiv 0 \mod |g_2|$ , and such $n$ exists because the equation can be rewritten as $p|g_1| + 1= q|g_2|$ while the Bezout's identity guarantee a solution since $\gcd(|g_1|, |g_2|) = 1$ . Therefore $g_1 \in H_1$ $\forall (g_1,g_2) \in K$ . Similarly $g_2 \in H_2$ , and then $(g_1, g_2) \in K \implies (g_1, g_2) \in H_1 \times H_2$ . $\square$ 
   
   (iii) Let $G_1 = G_2 = \Z_2$ , $K = \langle(1,1) \rangle$ , then $H_1 = H_2 = \{ 0 \}$ and $K \ne H_2 \times H_2$ . $\square$
